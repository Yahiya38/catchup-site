How we screen

Scope over titles.

This is the one idea CatchUp is built around. A shortlist built on titles, years and project value returns volume and almost no signal. Here is what we do instead.

The order we read a profile

  1. Employer type: general contractor, self perform contractor, specialty subcontractor, consultancy, owner's representative or design firm.
  2. What that company self performed.
  3. What the person owned inside it.
  4. Sector.
  5. Depth: how far into commissioning or closeout they stayed.
  6. Only then, title and years.

A tool or a recruiter that reads title and years first has the order upside down.

Three questions

  • What did the company self perform, versus manage subcontractors?
  • What did the person personally own, versus route or review?
  • Where did they hand off: substantial completion, or through integrated systems testing?

Seven questions, in writing, before any call

Writing comes first because these filter faster than a conversation does.

Hamza to confirm these can be published.

  1. Who did you work for, and what did that company self perform on your projects?
  2. On your most recent project, what did you personally own and what was owned by someone else?
  3. Did you write and negotiate change orders, or route them for approval?
  4. Where did your involvement stop: handoff before commissioning, or through integrated systems testing?
  5. Are you based in Texas, and if not, what is your specific relocation position?
  6. Are you legally authorized to work in the United States, and will you now or in the future require sponsorship?
  7. What percentage of travel can you sustain?

Mission critical, the dividing line

Commercial and mission critical projects of identical value look identical on a resume. They diverge at the point where systems have to prove they work.

On a commercial job, substantial completion is largely a punch list conversation. On a mission critical job it means Level 4 and Level 5 commissioning, integrated systems testing and load bank testing against redundancy requirements that do not move. The owner is not asking whether the building is finished. They are asking whether it fails safely.

So the question we ask is how far into commissioning someone stayed, not whether they have data center experience. Many strong project managers have built on data center sites without touching critical systems: shell and core, sitework, admin fit out. Good work, different job.

When we say it is not a fit

We tell people plainly when a background does not match a contractor side role.

  • Design side or engineer of record experience only.
  • Owner's representative or corporate real estate experience only.
  • Reviewed rather than owned RFIs and change orders.
  • No independent cost forecasting.
  • A vague relocation position on a site based role.
  • Travel tolerance below what the role needs.
